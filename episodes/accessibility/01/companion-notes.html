Companion reference

Accessibility preserves ways to perceive, understand, and operate a task. These notes introduce the mental model; they are not a complete conformance checklist.

One checkout, different access needs

  • Vision: readable text, enlargement, useful contrast, structure, and appropriate text alternatives. Screen readers and magnification address different needs and may be used together.
  • Hearing: important sound has an appropriate alternative; captions cover speech and meaningful audio in video, and transcripts provide another route to audio content.
  • Mobility and dexterity: keyboard paths, forgiving targets, and alternatives to precision gestures. Switch devices, voice control, and alternative keyboards are valid input methods.
  • Cognition and learning: clear instructions, consistent terms, predictable behavior, and recovery that does not needlessly depend on memory.
  • Speech: a service should not unnecessarily require speaking as the only path.
  • Vestibular and motion sensitivity: avoid unnecessary movement and provide appropriate controls and reduced-motion behavior.

People can have overlapping needs. Temporary and situational barriers help expose design assumptions; they are not equivalent to the lived experience of disability.

POUR as review questions

Perceivable
Can the person receive the order details, instructions, and results?
Operable
Can they reach and use the controls with their input method?
Understandable
Do the instructions, actions, and recovery paths make sense?
Robust
Does the page communicate information in ways browsers and assistive technologies can interpret?

WCAG means Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. Levels A, AA, and AAA describe increasing sets of requirements; AA includes A. Passing selected checks does not establish conformance, and conformance and usability are not identical.

A task-based review prompt

  1. Identify the person's goal, including its successful outcome.
  2. List essential information and actions, including errors and recovery.
  3. Find assumptions about perception, input, precision, memory, and timing.
  4. Record what you have actually evaluated and what remains assumed.
